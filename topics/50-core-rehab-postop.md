### Post-op exercises slide (Sun 22 Nov, Day 3 rehab)
**Slide (sp):** Exercises: ankle pumps (alternate feet); isometrics for quads, hams and glutes (quad setting: tighten thigh, push back of knee downward); heel slides; rotations till neutral (supine, IR/ER up to 45 deg each side from neutral 0); gravity-eliminated hip abduction (supine, slide leg out on the bed); active quadriceps (seated knee extension).
**Inputs (ad):**
- Ankle pumps = DVT prophylaxis (calf muscle pump), start in recovery/day 0.
- Quad setting/SLR-type work = quad inhibition (arthrogenic muscle inhibition) fight; glute sets for hip abductor/extensor recruitment.
- Heel slides = early flexion ROM (TKA) and gentle hip/knee flexion (THA, keep <90 deg hip flexion).
- Rotations till neutral: ROM only to neutral/45 deg, not beyond: respect approach precautions.
- Gravity-eliminated abduction: abductors (glut med) re-education, esp. after lateral approach; slide on bed, do not cross midline.
- Active seated knee extension: terminal extension/quad strength; in TKA watch extensor lag.
**Confirm with speaker:** whether the slide is THA only or THA+TKA; the sets/reps; when to progress to resisted.

### Stairs mnemonic (speaker, Day 3 rehab)
**Speaker (sp):** "Good leg goes to heaven, bad leg goes to hell."
**Meaning (ad):** Stairs with crutches/cane after THA/TKA: UP = good (non-operated) leg first, then operated leg, then crutch. DOWN = crutch first, then operated (bad) leg, then good leg. Standard teaching: "up with the good, down with the bad".

### Cane in the OPPOSITE hand after THA / in AVN hip, and the latissimus dorsi logic (Devarsh asked, Sun 22 Nov) [standard biomechanics + researched; lat dorsi link is my reading, confirm what the speaker said]
**Rule:** hold the cane in the hand OPPOSITE to the painful or operated hip.
**Why (moment arithmetic):** in single-leg stance the abductors of the stance hip balance body weight (abductor lever ~5-7 cm vs body-weight lever longer). Force through a cane on the opposite side acts at a lever of several tens of cm from the stance hip, so a small cane force (people carry ~1/6 body weight) cancels a large part of the body-weight moment. Less abductor force needed, so less joint reaction force (JRF = abductor force + body weight - cane force component).
- Textbook model numbers (Inman-type): no cane abductor force ~1.6 W, JRF ~2.4 W; cane SAME side 1.3 W / 2.0 W; cane OPPOSITE side 0.6 W / 1.3 W.
- THA gait study (Ajemian 2004): contralateral cane lowered the operated hip abduction moment by ~26% but RAISED the other hip's by ~28%. Cane EMG study: hip abductor activity lowest with maximal weight through a contralateral cane (66% of baseline) vs 424% with maximal weight through an ipsilateral cane.
- Cane on the same side: cane is near the stance hip so lever is short; the patient has to lean over it, abductors work harder.
**Latissimus dorsi (my reading of the speaker's point):** the arm is fixed on the cane (closed chain), so lat dorsi (origin: thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, lower ribs; insertion humerus) pulls the PELVIS on the cane side UP toward the arm. Right hip operated, cane in LEFT hand: left lat dorsi contraction hikes the left hemipelvis, resisting the left pelvic drop that happens in right stance. That does part of the job of the right gluteus medius. Caveat: lat dorsi is one of several load transmitters (triceps, shoulder depressors, lower trapezius, pectoralis major); literature explains the benefit mainly by the external moment of the cane force, not by naming one muscle.
**Practical:** use until Trendelenburg sign/limp resolves (usually weeks, surgeon-specific; confirm local protocol), cane height at greater trochanter level, move cane with the operated leg. Watch the opposite hip: load rises (relevant if contralateral hip has OA or a THA).
**AVN hip:** same rule offloads the affected femoral head, but a cane alone gives limited offloading; pre-collapse hips usually get crutches with protected/toe-touch weight-bearing. Meta-analysis (Front Surg 2022; 14 studies, 813 patients, 1025 hips, mostly observational): protective weight-bearing group THA rate 40%, collapse rate 46%; outcomes not significantly different from surgical hip preservation at short to mid term, but evidence quality is low.

### Bed transfer after THA: get out from the SAME side as the operated hip (Devarsh asked, Sun 22 Nov) [standard rehab teaching; confirm the speaker's reason and local protocol]
- Why: leaving from the operated side lets the operated leg slide off the bed edge in abduction without crossing the midline (avoids adduction and internal rotation, the posterior-dislocation position after a posterior approach). The non-operated leg stays on the bed and does the pushing.
- Sequence (out of bed): scoot toward the operated edge, keep the operated leg slightly abducted, slide it off first, sit up using hands and the good leg, stand on the good leg first, then take the walker/cane (cane in the hand OPPOSITE the operated hip). Getting in is the reverse: sit, good leg up first, then lift the operated leg with a leg lifter/strap or hand, no twisting.
- Other points: bed height such that hips are not below knees (>90 deg flexion), no pivoting on the operated leg, pillow between knees when lying on the back or the non-operated side.
- Approach matters: strict precautions apply mainly to the posterior approach; direct anterior/lateral protocols differ (many centres now use fewer restrictions). Follow the surgeon's protocol.

### Post-THA ADL modification (slide, Sun 22 Nov; slide photo saved as img/adl_slide.jpg; book figures 6-11, 6-16)
**Slide:** ADL Modification. (1) Bending to pick an object. (2) Sitting with ankle on knee / lying with ankles crossed. (3) Leaning forward while sit-to-stand transitions. (4) Reaching for the blanket near the feet. Bottom: Raise bed/chair heights. Avoid pivoting.
**Added (why, assuming posterior-approach precautions; confirm which approach the speaker meant):**
- Common thread: each movement takes the hip into deep flexion (>90 deg), adduction and/or internal rotation, the position that levers the femoral head out of the cup posteriorly after a posterior approach (capsule/short external rotators still healing).
- Bending to pick an object: trunk flexion over a fixed femur = hip flexion well beyond 90 deg. Use a reacher/grabber, or squat with the operated leg back and keep the back straight.
- Ankle on knee / ankles crossed: figure-of-4 gives flexion + abduction + external rotation when seated, but crossing at the ankles or knees adds ADDUCTION across the midline plus flexion; the slide groups it as to avoid. Keep a pillow between knees in bed and the feet apart.
- Leaning forward on sit-to-stand: the trunk folds over the thigh (flexion >90), and the pelvis tilts. Scoot forward, push up from the armrests, extend the operated leg out in front, keep the trunk upright.
- Reaching for a blanket near the feet: long-sitting forward reach = hip flexion with trunk flexion. Use a long-handled grabber or ask for help.
- Raise bed/chair/toilet heights (use a raised seat): keeps hips higher than knees so flexion stays under 90 deg and makes standing easier.
- Avoid pivoting: turning on a planted operated foot internally rotates the femur on the pelvis. Take small steps and turn the whole body.
- Link with the earlier bed-transfer and cane notes.

### No cross-legged sitting after TKA (user, Sun 22 Nov)
**User point:** after TKR, no cross-legged sitting.
**Inputs (ad):** cross-legged/squatting = deep knee flexion (~130-150 deg) + rotation and varus/valgus torque; high posterior-poly and patellofemoral contact stress, posterior condylar impingement, liftoff risk; early on also stresses healing soft tissue and swelling. Most TKA implants are designed for ~120-135 deg. "High-flex" designs have not shown proven benefit. Indian context: floor sitting, squatting toilet, prayer posture: needs counselling, raised/western toilet, chair or low stool. Duration of restriction and whether permanent varies by surgeon: confirm with speaker.

### Knee brace after TKA, evidence (user ask, Sun 22 Nov)
**Search finding:** JAAOS Global 2021 (Kemker, Golladay) hip/knee bracing systematic review: braces lack high-level evidence; sole bracing was the right answer only in MCL suture fixation during TKA. Found only small/ongoing RCTs on post-TKA braces (proprioceptive, OCSI), no robust meta-analysis showing routine benefit. 
**Inputs (ad, from teaching, not searched):** routine brace after primary TKA not supported. Possible uses: knee immobiliser for extensor lag / quad weakness when walking (temporary), intra-op MCL injury/repair, ligament laxity, hinged brace with constrained/hinged implant, patella/extensor repair. Risks: stiffness, quad atrophy, skin issues, delayed rehab. Confirm with speaker.
