## Big picture

In a child the ACL is **stronger than the growing bone** it attaches to. So when the knee is forced into hyperextension or a twisting load, the bone gives way and the ACL comes off *with a flake of tibial eminence*, instead of tearing in its middle.

> [!samajh] Bachche mein haddi ligament se kamzor hoti hai, isliye ligament toot'ta nahi, haddi ka tukda ligament ke saath nikal jaata hai. Adult mein ulta: ligament mid-substance se phategi.

- Peak age about 8 to 14 years (cycle fall, sports, twisting).
- Adults: rare; usually high-energy, can be associated with other ligament injuries.
- It is an **ACL injury equivalent**. Treat the instability, not just the "fracture".

## Presentation and exam {revise}

- Fall or twist, immediate **hemarthrosis** (tense swollen knee), unable to bear weight.
- **Extension block**: the knee will not go fully straight. Think displaced fragment or entrapped meniscus (anterior horn, or intermeniscal ligament).
- Lachman / anterior drawer: may be positive but is painful and unreliable in the acute stage. Do not force it in a child.
- Look for associated injury: collateral ligaments, meniscus, distal femoral or proximal tibial physeal injury.

> [!flag] Neurovascular check always. A tense haemarthrosis plus extension block is a displaced fragment until proven otherwise.

## Investigations

- **X-ray knee AP + lateral**: the lateral shows the elevated fragment best. Look at how far the front of the fragment is lifted.
- **MRI**: helps when the X-ray is unclear, and shows meniscal entrapment and associated injuries. Fragment in a child is often mostly cartilage, so the bony flake underestimates the size.
- Aspiration of the haemarthrosis relieves pain and helps the exam.

## Meyers and McKeever classification {revise}

| Type | What you see | Usual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| I | Minimal displacement (edge only) | Cast or brace |
| II | Anterior third lifted, hinged posteriorly (beaked) | Try closed reduction, else surgery |
| III | Fully displaced from its bed (IIIA fragment only, IIIB rotated) | Surgery |
| IV (Zaricznyj) | Comminuted | Surgery |

> [!verify] Some books treat Type II as cast only if the fragment reduces fully in extension on X-ray, else arthroscopic fixation. Confirm your book's cut-off and the usual cast flexion angle.

## Treatment

### Type I (and a reduced Type II)
- Aspirate if tense. Long-leg cast or brace in **slight flexion** (about 10 to 20 degrees). Full extension can tension the fragment wrongly.
- Recheck X-ray at 1 to 2 weeks to confirm it has not displaced.
- Total immobilisation about 4 to 6 weeks, then ROM and strengthening.

> [!verify] Exact flexion angle in the cast (some say 0 to 20, others 20) and duration. Check your book.

### Type II (not reducing), III, IV
- **Arthroscopic reduction and fixation**.
- Fixation options: suture through the base of ACL and tibial tunnels (physeal friendly), screw (epiphyseal only), or anchors.
- Clear any entrapped meniscus or intermeniscal ligament first.
- In a skeletally immature child, **do not cross the physis** with hardware if possible.

### Adult
- Rare. If the fragment is displaced, fix it. If the knee is unstable with a small fragment, manage like an ACL tear (reconstruct).

## After surgery

1. Brace, protected weight-bearing, early gentle ROM to prevent stiffness.
2. Sports return only after strength and stability tests, typically **months, not weeks**.

## Complications to name

- **Arthrofibrosis / stiffness** (most common).
- Residual laxity even when the bone heals (the ACL is stretched).
- Malunion with an extension block or impingement.
- Physeal injury and growth disturbance (rare, hardware related).

## Viva {viva}

:::qa Why does the tibial spine avulse instead of the ACL tearing in a child?
In a child the ACL is stronger than the immature, partly cartilaginous bone at its tibial footprint. The weak link fails first.
:::

:::qa Name the classification.
Meyers and McKeever types I to III, with Zaricznyj adding type IV (comminuted).
:::

:::qa What is the classic sign of a displaced fragment?
Haemarthrosis with a block to full extension.
:::

:::qa Why still follow up after the bone heals?
The ACL may be stretched, so laxity can remain even with a united fragment.
:::
