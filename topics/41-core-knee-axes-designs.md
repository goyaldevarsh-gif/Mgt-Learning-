## Talk 1: Surgical Anatomy of the Knee and Mechanical Axes
**Devarsh's notes**
- Medial femoral condyle sits lower (more distal) than the lateral condyle, in AP and lateral views.

**Added (standard text / evidence)**
- Distal femoral joint line is ~3 deg varus to the mechanical axis because medial condyle is more distal. Femoral mechanical axis (hip centre to knee centre) is ~87 deg to the distal femoral joint line (mLDFA, normal ~85-90).
- Proximal tibial joint line is also ~3 deg varus (MPTA ~87). The two cancel out, so the whole-limb mechanical axis is neutral (HKA ~180) and the joint line is parallel to the floor in stance.
- Posterior condyles: the medial posterior condyle is bigger, so the posterior condylar axis is ~3 deg internally rotated relative to the surgical transepicondylar axis. This is the basis of the 3 deg external rotation set in femoral component rotation.
- Anatomical vs mechanical axis of femur differ by ~5-7 deg, which sets the valgus angle of the intramedullary distal femoral cut guide.

- Reason for 3 deg varus (Devarsh): knee adduction moment arm.
  Added: In single-limb stance GRF passes medial to knee centre, so GRF x perpendicular distance (the adductor arm) = external knee adduction moment (KAM), loading the medial compartment (~60-70% of load in a neutral knee, Hsu 1990). Varus malalignment increases the arm and the load; it is a key driver of medial OA progression. Constitutional varus >=3 deg is seen in ~32% men, 17% women (Bellemans 2012) - the basis of "kinematic / personalised alignment" discussions later.

## Slide: Distal Femoral Cut
**Slide content:** Valgus cut angle determined from preop XR (default 5-6 deg valgus) = angle between anatomic axis (AAF) and mechanical axis (MAF) of femur. Valgus cut angle = AAF - MAF. Distal femoral cut is perpendicular to MAF. Diagram shows 6 deg (AAF-MAF) and 3 deg (joint line vs. perpendicular).
**Added:**
- Measure on a long-leg / full-length standing AP with neutral rotation (patella forward); a short knee film mis-estimates the axis.
- Set IM guide to measured angle (usually 5 or 6 deg; some use 7 for tall/ coxa vara etc.). Entry point just anterior to PCL origin, medial to the intercondylar notch centre.
- Pitfalls: femoral bowing (lateral), prior fracture/malunion, hip deformity or IM canal obstruction (stem) -> IM guide unreliable; use extramedullary / navigation / robotic. Wrong valgus angle = femoral component malalignment (varus if angle too small). Shortened femur: use patient-specific angle rather than default.
- Reference: ~1 deg of error in distal cut angle matters, but malalignment >3 deg of mechanical axis was classically linked to early failure (Jeffery 1991, Ritter 1994); newer data (Parratte 2010, Bellemans 2012) questions this. 

## Talk 2: Knee Biomechanics and TKA Designs
**Slide: TKA design (cruciate ligaments, articular geometry & constraint)**
- Non-constrained: bicruciate-retaining (struck off by speaker), cruciate-retaining (CR, circled), anterior-stabilized CR.
- Intermediate constraint: medial pivot, anterior stabilized, bicruciate stabilized. Speaker circled this group and wrote "CS (PS)": the posterior-stabilized / cruciate-substituting design belongs here.
- Constrained: non-hinge TKA (CCK).
- Highly constrained: hinge TKA.
**Added:**
- CR: PCL preserved, needs a functioning PCL and balanced flexion gap; flat/dished insert with tibial slope (~3-7 deg) driving rollback. Risks: tight PCL (reduced flexion, wear), laxity.
- PS/CS: post-cam replaces PCL, forces femoral rollback, easier gap balancing in deformity/inflammatory arthritis/post-patellectomy/PCL-deficient knees. Risks: patellar clunk (fibrous nodule on femoral box), post wear/fracture, box resects more distal femoral bone, spine jump in flexion with malrotation/laxity.
- CR vs PS: no consistent difference in survival or function in RCTs/registries (Cochrane; Verra 2013 meta-analysis), so choice follows surgeon familiarity and knee pathology. PS has slightly better flexion in some series.
- Constraint ladder: use the least constraint that gives a stable knee. CCK for MCL insufficiency or large flexion/extension gap mismatch; hinge for global instability, bone loss (tumour, revision).
- Medial pivot: ball-in-socket medial compartment, flat/ lax lateral, mimics native knee kinematics; evidence is early/limited.


### Joint line restoration: how much change is tolerated? (speaker: max ~4 mm up or down)
**Speaker point (Devarsh's note):** joint line change up to ~4 mm is tolerated, either elevation or depression; more than that is not.
**Evidence I found (researched):**
- Systematic review, KSSTA 2019;27:1477-86 (27 studies, 8 in correlation analysis; Level IV): mean JL elevation 3.0 mm after primary and 3.6 mm after revision TKA; significant negative correlation between JL elevation and post-op KSS function (rho -0.496); authors conclude elevation should not exceed 4 mm.
- Prospective study, 120 primary TKAs (PMC9490095): mean elevation 3.0 +/- 2.1 mm; elevation >=5 mm had worse functional scores at 1 yr (WOMAC/KSS) than <5 mm.
- Cadaver study (Clin Orthop Relat Res 2018;476:601-11; PMC6260050): medial JL raised 2 mm -> mid-flexion (30 deg) laxity +64% (about 3.1 deg); raised 4 mm -> +111% (about 5.4 deg) vs the 9 mm baseline resection. No significant difference in extension, 90 or 120 deg. (So instability is mid-flexion, the 30-60 deg range.)
- Mid-flexion instability review (PMC4849283): JL elevation alters the flexion-extension axis -> laxity of posterior capsule, PCL and collaterals at 30-60 deg; a cadaver CR-TKA study showed PCL strain rising once elevation exceeds ~2 mm; elevation also linked to anterior knee pain and reduced flexion via patellofemoral mechanics.
- Squatting ability study (Ther Clin Risk Manag): JL position and pre-op ROM were independent factors for ability to squat after TKA.
- Accuracy in conventional TKA (J Clin Med 2025;14:1264, n=115): 92.2% had JL restored within 4 mm (adductor tubercle to JL); valgus knees showed significant elevation, varus knees did not.
- Flexion contracture review (PMC9947997): pre-planned extra distal femoral resection to fix FFD causes JL elevation, mid-flexion instability and pseudo-patella baja.
**Added (practical):**
- 4 mm is a pragmatic threshold from low-level evidence (mostly retrospective/correlation, JL elevation); it is not a hard cut-off. Evidence on depression (lowering) is thinner; the "either direction" rule is the speaker's/standard teaching, mid-flexion and patellar effects are best documented for elevation.
- How to keep the JL: distal femoral cut = implant thickness (about 9 mm) corrected for cartilage/bone wear, not extra "to be safe"; do not recut the distal femur to solve flexion contracture (release posterior capsule/osteophytes first); use distal femoral augments if bone is deficient; avoid over-thick insert as a way to gain stability; watch for pseudo-patella baja (Insall-Salvati / Blackburne-Peel); valgus knees are the ones prone to elevation (hypoplastic lateral condyle).
- Linking: matches the Day 1 distal-femur cut slide ("resection thickness = implant thickness, joint line unchanged").
