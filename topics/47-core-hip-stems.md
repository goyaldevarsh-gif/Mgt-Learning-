### Cemented femoral stems: composite beam vs taper-slip (polished tapered) [standard text; Devarsh asked for the table, confirm against the speaker's slide]
| Feature | Composite beam | Taper-slip (polished tapered) |
|---|---|---|
| Fixation principle | Stem bonded to cement, cement bonded to bone; stem+cement act as one composite; NO motion at the stem-cement interface | Stem is debonded, polished, tapered; it is allowed to subside a little within the mantle, the wedge then loads the cement with compressive hoop stresses (force-closed, "self-tightening") |
| Surface finish | Rough / matt (often grit-blasted, pre-coated or textured) | Highly polished (mirror finish) |
| Shape | Usually straight/parallel-sided, often collared | Double- or triple-tapered, collarless |
| Subsidence | Not intended (any movement = debonding = failure sign) | Intended and tolerated (about 1-2 mm of subsidence/creep) |
| Cement behaviour | Cement must stay bonded; stress concentrates at the stem-cement interface | Cement creeps and relaxes the stresses; mantle quality is the key |
| Typical failure mode | Debonding -> micromotion -> cement fracture, abrasion debris, osteolysis | Poor mantle / malalignment / thin cement -> excessive subsidence or rotation; rough surface turns it into a bad design |
| Examples (commonly quoted) | Charnley, Muller straight stem, Iowa | Exeter, CPT, Lubinus SP II |
| Technical pearls | Needs a good cement-stem bond (clean, dry stem; sometimes PMMA pre-coat); canal and cement pressurisation | No pre-coat or roughening; do not use a collar; ensure a complete 2-5 mm cement mantle all round with a distal centraliser; size for the mantle, not canal fill |
| Registry impression | Good long-term results in classic designs (Charnley) | Excellent registry survival for polished taper-slip designs (e.g. Exeter); matt/rough stems tend to do worse in some national registries (check data before quoting numbers) |

**Why taper-slip is considered better (standard teaching; evidence is registry/observational, not RCT):**
- Designed to tolerate cement creep: the polished stem subsides 1-2 mm, the taper wedges in and keeps loading the cement in compression (hoop stress) instead of letting it crack.
- No stem-cement bond to lose: composite-beam stems fail once debonding starts (micromotion, abrasion debris, cement fracture, osteolysis). A polished stem moving against cement makes little debris, and the cement mantle survives.
- Forgiving of the surgeon: stem finds its own stable position in the mantle; bond quality depends less on technique (clean/dry stem, pre-coat).
- Track record: Exeter-type polished taper-slip stems have excellent long-term survival in registries (e.g. NJR, Scandinavian), generally better than matt/rough cemented stems in several national registries. Check exact numbers before quoting.
- Caveats: needs a complete 2-5 mm mantle all round (distal centraliser), no collar, no roughening/pre-coat; not suited if the mantle is poor or the canal is very wide/irregular; classic composite-beam designs (e.g. Charnley) also have excellent long-term results.

### Cementless stems: bone ingrowth vs ongrowth, and stress shielding [standard text; confirm against speaker's slides]
| | Ingrowth (porous) | Ongrowth (rough/coated) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Bone grows INTO a porous 3D surface | Bone grows ONTO a roughened or coated surface (osseointegration) |
| Surfaces | Sintered beads (CoCr/Ti), fibre-metal mesh, porous tantalum/trabecular metal, 3D-printed lattices | Grit-blasted Ti, plasma-sprayed Ti, hydroxyapatite (HA) coating (HA gives bone bonding) |
| Needs | Pore size ~100-400 um, porosity ~30-50%, tight press-fit (gap about <0.5 mm), micromotion below ~40 um (>150 um = only fibrous tissue) | Tight press-fit, micromotion low (<~150 um), good contact; HA speeds early fixation |
| Typical stems | Extensively porous-coated (e.g. AML type), beaded proximal | Grit-blast/HA proximal-coated tapered (e.g. Corail type) |
| Strength | Strong mechanical interlock | Reliable, less demanding; HA helps in poor bone |
| Problem | If coated distally/extensively: proximal stress shielding, thigh pain; revision is harder | Fixation is weaker than ingrowth in lab, but clinically very successful |
**Which is better?** No clear winner: both work well. Registry and long-term data favour modern proximally coated (metaphyseal-engaging) tapered stems (grit-blast +/- HA, or porous proximal). What matters more than "ingrowth vs ongrowth" is WHERE the fixation is and HOW STIFF the stem is.
**Stress shielding (Wolff's law: bone remodels to the load it sees):**
- Mechanism: a stiff stem carries load past the proximal femur; the proximal bone is unloaded and resorbs (Gruen zones 1 and 7, calcar). Grades per Engh classification (1-4).
- Determinants: (1) stem stiffness (rises steeply with diameter, about to the 4th power; CoCr ~210 GPa > Ti ~110 GPa > cortical bone ~17-20 GPa); (2) extent/site of coating (extensive/distal fixation = more shielding); (3) canal fill and stem size (big, canal-filling stems); (4) bone quality/age.
- Link: Extensive porous (ingrowth) coating used to fix distally -> distal load transfer -> proximal shielding + thigh pain. Proximal-only coating (ingrowth or ongrowth) lets the metaphysis share load -> less shielding.
- Ways to reduce: proximal (metaphyseal) coating, tapered or short/metaphyseal-fitting stems, titanium or lower-modulus (e.g. cut-out, hollow, TMT) designs, not oversizing the stem.
- Reality check: radiographic stress shielding often does not correlate with pain or early failure, but it matters for revision bone stock.

### Stress shielding in THA (focused summary) [standard text; confirm against slides]
- Definition: bone resorbs when an implant takes over load that the bone used to carry (Wolff's law; strain-energy-density concept). Seen as proximal femoral bone loss around a stable cementless stem.
- Where: Gruen zones 1 (greater trochanter) and 7 (calcar, medial), spreading to 2 and 6 in severe cases. Mostly in the first 1-2 years, then plateaus.
- Why: the stem (stiff metal) in a rigid distal fit carries the load; proximal bone is bypassed and under-strained.
- Determinants: stem stiffness (diameter to the 4th power; CoCr > Ti > bone), extent of distal fixation / coating, canal-filling oversize stems, stem length, low bone quality (older, osteoporotic), cementless fixation (cemented stems transfer load more evenly).
- Grading (Engh): degree 1 calcar rounding, 2 calcar resorption to lesser trochanter, 3 cortical atrophy below lesser trochanter, 4 extending toward distal stem (approx. wording).
- Clinical importance: usually asymptomatic; may link to thigh pain (also from stiffness mismatch); main concerns are loss of bone stock for revision and higher fracture risk in the weakened proximal femur. Radiographic shielding often does not predict stem loosening or poor function.
- Prevention: metaphyseal (proximal) fixation, tapered or short stems, titanium or lower-modulus/porous-trabecular designs, avoid oversizing, preserve bone (neck-preserving short stems).
- Viva one-liner: "Stiff, distally fixed, canal-filling stems unload the proximal femur (Gruen 1 and 7) so it resorbs by Wolff's law; I choose a proximally fixed tapered titanium stem sized to bone, not oversized."

### Cementless femoral stems by shape (Khanuja 2011 JBJS, modified from Berry 2000) [researched]
| Type | Shape | Fixation site | Notes / examples (commonly quoted) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Single wedge (flat taper / "blade") | Metaphysis (medial-lateral, 3-point) | Often broach-only, no distal reaming; suits Dorr A (champagne-flute) femur; e.g. Taperloc, Accolade. Distal fit in Dorr A can cause rotational instability |
| 2 | Double wedge, metaphyseal filling | Metaphysis (cortical contact in ML and AP planes) | "Fit and fill"; e.g. Corail-type |
| 3A | Tapered round | Metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction | |
| 3B | Tapered spline / cone | Metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction | Splines/ridges give rotational stability; e.g. Wagner Cone-type |
| 3C | Tapered rectangle | Metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction | e.g. Zweymuller-type |
| 4 | Cylindrical, fully (extensively) coated | Diaphysis ("scratch fit") | e.g. AML; needs precise reaming; more stress shielding and thigh pain |
| 5 | Modular (neck-body or body-stem) | Variable (usually diaphysis) | e.g. S-ROM; for dysplasia, deformity, revision; extra junction = fretting/corrosion risk |
| 6 | Anatomic | Metaphysis | Left/right-specific; matches proximal canal |
Memory hook: 1, 2, 6 = metaphysis; 3 = metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction; 4 = diaphysis.
Other schemes: Callaghan (7 groups: modular, extensively coated, HA coated, proximally ingrown, tapered, press-fit, custom); descriptive grouping into cylindrical / tapered (2D flat vs 3D double-tapered) / anatomic.
Link: fixation site drives load transfer and stress shielding (proximal = less shielding, diaphyseal = more) and which Dorr femur type suits which stem (Dorr A: single wedge; Dorr C: consider cemented or specific stems).
Short/neck-preserving stems have their own classification (Khanuja 2014, Types I-III by fixation principle); not covered here.

### Primary vs secondary fixation in cementless stems [standard text; confirm against speaker's slide]
| | Primary (mechanical) stability | Secondary (biological) stability |
|---|---|---|
| What | Immediate stability from the press-fit at surgery | Bone ingrowth/ongrowth (osseointegration) that follows |
| Source | Interference fit: wedge/taper geometry, 3-point contact, cortical contact, rotational profile (rectangular/spline) | Bone bonds to porous (ingrowth) or rough/HA-coated (ongrowth) surface |
| When | At implantation, first weeks | Develops over about 6-12 weeks, matures over months |
| Depends on | Canal preparation (broach/ream accuracy), stem geometry, bone quality (Dorr type), size match (neither under- nor oversized), surgeon technique | Primary stability (prerequisite), micromotion, surface (pore size, HA), bone-implant contact, host biology (age, osteoporosis, smoking, steroids) |
| Targets | Micromotion low enough for bone to form (about <150 um at most; ideal ~<40 um) | Stable bony fixation; stem stops moving |
| Failure | Under-sized or poorly seated: subsidence, rotation, thigh pain; oversized: intra-op periprosthetic fracture | Only fibrous (not bony) fixation: persistent pain, late subsidence/loosening |
**Key points:**
- Primary stability is the prerequisite for secondary; no primary = fibrous tissue instead of bone.
- Intra-op checks: stem seated to planned depth and version, no rotation or toggle on trial, ring/"pitch" change on final impaction, no fracture.
- Early subsidence of 1-2 mm is common and acceptable; ongoing subsidence is not.
- Surface treatments (porous, HA) and good bone help secondary; HA helps especially in poorer bone.
- Weight-bearing: modern stems with good primary fit often allow early/full weight-bearing; protect weight-bearing if fit is doubtful or a calcar/greater trochanter fracture occurred.
- Cemented comparison: cemented stems have immediate fixation (cement interlock) and no secondary biological phase.
