## Major takeaways
> [!tip] Sabse zaroori 7 points
> 1. **Templating sirf TRUE AP pelvis par.** Inlet/outlet views templating ke liye nahi (wo pelvic trauma views hain). Rotation ya tilt se offset, neck length, LLD aur cup angles galat padte hain.
> 2. **Magnification ~120%.** True size = measured / 1.2. Obese mein zyada ho sakta hai, isliye marker se check karo.
> 3. **Marker calibration:** 30 mm ball film par 36 mm dikhe to 1.2. Ya 10 cm K-wire 12 cm dikhe to 1.2. Marker thigh par greater trochanter level par (hip ke same plane mein).
> 4. **Teardrop** cup medialisation ka landmark hai par COR ke liye limited validity (pelvic tilt se badalta hai). Pooch lena ki speaker "types of teardrop" se kya matlab tha.
> 5. **Cup target:** inclination ~40°, anteversion ~15-20° (Lewinnek safe zone 40±10, 15±10). **Reaming bhi anteversion mein**, planned cup orientation ke saath, warna eccentric bed.
> 6. **Cup fixation:** primary = press-fit (1-2 mm under-ream). Screws sirf doubtful press-fit, osteoporotic/sclerotic bone, dysplasia, revision mein. Screw **posterior-superior** quadrant mein, anterior quadrants danger.
> 7. **Cup lateralise karne ke fayde:** global offset, soft-tissue tension bina leg lengthening, kam impingement, medial bone stock save. Cost: joint reaction force badhta hai, uncovered cup ya iliopsoas impingement ka risk.

> [!flag] Common galtiyan
> - Rotated ya tilted pelvis par templating (symmetrical obturator foramina aur coccyx-symphysis alignment check karo).
> - Magnification ko ignore karna ya template aur film ka factor mismatch.
> - Deep medial reaming se offset aur medial wall kho dena.

:::qa Templating ke liye inlet/outlet chahiye?
Nahi. Sirf true AP pelvis (aur zaroorat par hip AP/lateral). Inlet/outlet pelvic trauma ke views hain.
:::

:::qa Magnification kaise confirm karte ho?
Known marker hip level par: 30 mm ball 36 mm dikhe to 1.2. 10 cm K-wire 12 cm dikhe to 1.2. True size = measured / 1.2.
:::

:::qa Acetabulum ream kis direction mein?
Anteversion mein, planned cup orientation (~40° inclination, ~15-20° anteversion) ke saath, pehle chhota reamer medially floor tak, phir badhate hue.
:::

:::qa Screw kahan lagate ho aur kahan nahi?
Posterior-superior safe. Anterior-superior (external iliac vessels) aur anterior-inferior (obturator) dangerous. Posterior-inferior mein chhote screw.
:::


### Acetabular cup: screw fixation and other supplemental fixation points [standard text; "point fixation" interpreted as supplemental fixation points (screws/spikes/pegs/fins); confirm what the speaker meant]
**Base rule:** primary fixation is press-fit (under-ream 1-2 mm, cup 1-2 mm larger than final reamer in good bone; 2 mm+ risks fracture in osteoporotic bone); screws/spikes/pegs are adjuncts for initial stability, not a substitute for a good bony fit. Secondary fixation = bone ingrowth into the porous shell.
**When to add screws:** doubtful press-fit (levering/rocking on trial), osteoporotic or sclerotic bone, dysplasia with incomplete cover, segmental/cavitary defects, revision, high-risk for early migration. Not needed if cup is rock-stable.
**Screws: pros/cons:** extra initial stability; but holes allow polyethylene debris to reach the back side (osteolysis), risk to neurovascular structures, cost/time, screw-liner fretting; no proven long-term survival benefit when press-fit is good.
**Safe zones (Wasielewski quadrants):** line from ASIS through the centre of the acetabulum, plus a perpendicular line, gives 4 quadrants.
- Posterior-superior: safest, best bone (supra-acetabular/iliac), longest screws (about 25-35 mm).
- Posterior-inferior: relatively safe but keep screws short (sciatic nerve, inferior gluteal and internal pudendal vessels).
- Anterior-superior: DANGEROUS (external iliac artery and vein; also femoral nerve).
- Anterior-inferior: DANGEROUS (obturator nerve and vessels).
- Rule: aim screws into the posterior-superior quadrant; avoid the anterior quadrants; measure depth; use bicortical only when needed; avoid over-penetration.
**Other supplemental fixation points:** spikes/pegs/fins (dome/rim points of bone purchase; rotational stability, no extra holes); line-to-line/ under-ream press-fit; trabecular metal shells; cages/augments for large defects.
**Technique pearls:** check stability by lever/rock test, seat the cup until flush with the reamed bone, plug unused holes (or use hole-less shells), remember to check screw ends clinically/by xray.

### Pre-operative templating in THA: why it is needed [standard text; speaker point "Templating needed"; confirm slide details]
**Why:** restore hip biomechanics (centre of rotation, offset, leg length), predict cup/stem sizes and neck cut level, have the right implants ready (Dorr A/C femur, dysplasia, protrusio, deformity, previous surgery), spot problems early, reduce intra-op surprises. Intra-op findings always rule; template = plan A + plan B.
**What to template:** cup size and position (centre of rotation, inclination ~40 deg, anteversion ~15-20 deg, medialisation to the teardrop/ilioischial line), stem size, level of femoral neck cut, neck length, head offset (standard vs high-offset), expected leg-length change, need for special implants (cemented, dysplastic, modular, cone).
**Imaging needed:** AP pelvis (centred on the pubic symphysis, both hips, legs internally rotated about 15 deg so the neck is in profile, neutral pelvic tilt), AP and lateral of the hip; calibration marker (ball) or digital calibration for magnification (about 115-120% with ball/older films); full-length femur if deformity.
**Steps:** (1) magnification calibrated; (2) draw reference lines (inter-teardrop line, lesser trochanter level, contralateral hip as the template for offset and neck length); (3) acetabular template: place at the native COR, correct inclination, cover and medialisation; (4) femoral template: size by canal fill, set neck cut so leg length and offset match the contralateral side; (5) check LLD and offset; (6) record size ranges and backup implants.
**Limits:** magnification error, malrotated or tilted pelvis, flexion contracture, wrong side as template, bone quality at surgery; digital templating is faster and similar accuracy; templating predicts exact stem/cup size only in a fraction of cases, usually within one size.

**Speaker point: templating only on a TRUE AP view.**
- Criteria for a true AP pelvis (standard text): symmetrical obturator foramina and iliac wings (no rotation), tip of coccyx in line with the pubic symphysis and about 1-3 cm above it (no pelvic tilt), teardrops/ilioischial lines clearly seen; legs internally rotated ~15 deg so the femoral neck is seen in full length (offset and neck length).
- Why: pelvic rotation or tilt changes apparent cup inclination/anteversion, offset, neck length and leg length; a templating error (wrong stem size, offset, or LLD) follows. If the film is not a true AP, repeat it rather than template on it.

**Speaker point (clarification): inlet/outlet views are NOT needed for THA templating; only a TRUE AP pelvis is required.**
- Inlet/outlet views are fracture/pelvic-ring views (used in pelvis and acetabulum trauma); they are not the templating films.
- Templating set: true AP pelvis (+ AP/lateral of the hip as needed). Judo/oblique views only for specific problems (e.g. dysplasia or acetabular deficiency), not routine.

**Speaker point: usual magnification is ~120%.**
- Analogue films are typically magnified about 115-120% (source-to-film distance, object-to-film distance); the template must match (e.g. 120% templates). Magnification varies with patient size/BMI, so a calibration marker (25-30 mm ball/coin at the level of the hip, between the thighs) is used to measure the real factor; digital templating calibrates against the marker.
- Obese patients: the hip is further from the film, so true magnification is often higher than 120%; check with the marker.
- Formula: true size = measured size / magnification (e.g. 120% => divide by 1.2). Report the factor used in the plan.

**Speaker example (how to confirm 120%):** place a 30 mm sphere (marker ball) at hip level; if it measures 36 mm on the film, magnification = 36/30 = 1.2 = 120%. Use that factor (or the template of the same %) for all measurements. If the ball reads differently (e.g. 39 mm = 130%), use the measured factor.

**Speaker alternative marker:** a K-wire of known length can be used instead of a ball. Example: 10 cm K-wire measuring 12 cm on the film = 12/10 = 1.2 = 120% magnification. Place it at the level of the hip (between the thighs, parallel to the film), same height as the joint centre.

**Speaker point: where to put the marker.** Place the marker (ball or K-wire) on the thigh, or strap it to the thigh, at the level of the greater trochanter ("GY" typed; read as GT, confirm). The tip of the greater trochanter is roughly at the level of the femoral head centre, so the marker sits at the same distance from the film as the hip joint and gives the right magnification. Marker too anterior/low or too high gives a wrong factor.

### Teardrop (Koehler's teardrop figure) in THA templating [researched; "types of teardrop" classification NOT found]
- Radiographic teardrop (Koehler 1931): created by superimposition of several structures in the AP ray path; U-shaped figure along the medial acetabular surface; no single direct anatomical correlate. Its lateral limb is the inner wall of the acetabulum, the medial limb the quadrilateral surface/ilioischial area; its base lies near the true acetabular floor, inferomedially, just above the obturator foramen.
- Use: landmark for cup position and centre of rotation: medial edge of the acetabular template placed at/just lateral to the teardrop, inferior margin at the level of the obturator foramen (templating rule); inter-teardrop line is the usual pelvic reference for leg length (and for Tonnis angle).
- Limits: validity as a COR landmark is limited (2023 study of 250 films: KTF not a sufficiently valid COR landmark; its distance to the COR varies with pelvic tilt), so use together with the acetabular roof/dome and contralateral hip.
- Teardrop vs ilioischial (Koehler) line: teardrop is relatively constant and little affected by small rotation, the ilioischial line is more sensitive.
- I could not find an established classification of "types of teardrop". ASK the speaker/send the slide: which types did he mean (shape variants, or teardrop-ilioischial line relationships)?


### Benefits (and costs) of lateralising the acetabular cup in THA [standard text; confirm against speaker's slide]
**Meaning:** place the cup/head centre a little lateral (i.e. do not over-medialise by deep reaming into the medial wall; or use a lateralised/offset liner or a thicker shell) so the centre of rotation is at/near the native hip centre.
**Benefits:**
- Restores or increases GLOBAL OFFSET (femoral offset + acetabular offset): longer abductor lever arm, better abductor tension and gait.
- Better soft-tissue tension and stability without lengthening the leg (gain tension without a longer neck).
- Less impingement (bony and prosthetic) because the neck clears the rim and the femur/GT clear the pelvis.
- Preserves medial bone stock (less medial reaming; keeps the teardrop/medial wall), useful for revision.
- Avoids a "buried"/over-medialised cup with a medial protruding cup or loss of offset (a medialised cup lowers global offset).
**Costs/limits:**
- Lateralisation increases the lever arm of body weight about the hip: joint reaction force rises (medialising, the Charnley principle, lowers it): possible higher wear/loading.
- Lateral overhang/uncovered cup if too lateral (need adequate bony cover, ~70%+), iliopsoas impingement from a prominent anterior rim, trochanteric pain or tight soft tissues if overdone; large lateralisation (more than a few mm) can overstuff the joint.
- So: aim for the native COR (match the contralateral side); do not penetrate the medial wall (teardrop); get offset by a lateralised liner or offset stem rather than a very lateral cup; avoid medialisation beyond the ilioischial line/teardrop in good bone.

### Acetabular reaming: in anteversion or retroversion? [standard text; confirm against speaker's slide]
- Answer: ream in ANTEVERSION, in the same orientation as the planned cup: about 40-45 deg inclination (abduction) and about 15-20 deg anteversion (Lewinnek safe zone: inclination 40 +/- 10 deg, anteversion 15 +/- 10 deg). Never ream in retroversion.
- Why: the reamer cuts the bed for the cup; if the bed is made in one direction and the cup is impacted in another, you get an eccentric bed, wrong seating, poor cover and malposition. Reaming in the planned orientation makes the cup sit concentrically and gives uniform bony contact.
- Landmarks to set anteversion: transverse acetabular ligament (TAL) (cup rim parallel to it), anterior and posterior walls/rims, the ischium and the pubis (check by seeing the cup edge relative to the posterior wall).
- Steps: first reamer small, directed medially to the true floor (cotyloid fossa/teardrop), then enlarge in the planned direction with progressively larger reamers, check each time that the anterior and posterior walls are preserved; do not ream more than the template size; finish at the planned size and ream-direction.
- Pitfalls (hedged): over-anteverted reaming thins the posterior wall (posterior instability, loss of posterior cover); retroverted reaming risks the anterior wall/column and gives a retroverted cup (anterior impingement, posterior-edge loading/dislocation, iliopsoas problems). Wrong reaming also risks medial wall breach.
- Pelvic tilt/spinopelvic mobility changes the functional anteversion (Day 3 topic); the surgical target is the planned anatomical orientation.

**Devarsh's practical rule (reaming):** ream the acetabulum with the reamer directed/tilted anteriorly (anteversion). Clarification: the reamer shaft lies along the future cup axis, so the handle (outside) tilts toward the patient's front (anterior) by about 15-20 deg and is angled about 40-45 deg from the horizontal long axis (abduction). In lateral decubitus (posterior approach) the surgeon stands behind the patient and tilts the handle toward the patient's abdomen; for the direct anterior approach the surgeon stands in front and the reamer goes in from the front. Do not rely only on the handle angle: the pelvis moves in lateral decubitus; check against the TAL and the anterior/posterior walls.
