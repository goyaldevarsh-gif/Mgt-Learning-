## Talk 5: Bone Cuts in TKA - What, Why and How?
**Slide: Distal femur cut**
1. Mechanical alignment = calculate VCA (valgus cut angle).
2. Extension gap and joint line height = how much to cut.
**Added:**
- VCA decides the angle (see the earlier distal femoral cut slide: VCA = anatomic - mechanical axis angle, usually 5-6 deg), the resection thickness decides the extension gap and joint line height.
- Typical resection: equal to the thickness of the distal femoral implant (~8-10 mm, size dependent), measured from the most distal condyle (the reference point depends on the guide and which condyle is worn; confirm with the system's technique), with an allowance for cartilage/ bone wear. Taking less leaves a tight extension gap; taking more raises the joint line (gap too loose, mid-flexion instability, patella baja/ alta effect).
- Joint line: aim to restore it within ~3-5 mm of native; elevation >8 mm is linked with poor function/ instability. Fixed flexion deformity: recut distal femur a further 2 mm at a time only after posterior osteophytes/ capsule are cleared; do not rely on cutting more femur to fix FFD alone.
- Check extension gap rectangular (equal medial/ lateral) with the spacer before moving to the flexion gap.

## Slide: VCA - how to get it
- VCA: pre-op; per-op with "guesstimate".
- Image: anatomic axis vs mechanical axis = 5 deg; cutting slot perpendicular (90 deg) to the mechanical axis.
**Added:**
- Pre-op: measure on a full-length standing AP (hip to ankle, patella forward): draw the femoral mechanical axis (hip centre to knee centre) and the anatomical axis (mid-shaft line); VCA = angle between them. Normal range is wide (about 2-9 deg, mean ~5-7), so a fixed 5 deg is wrong for a meaningful minority.
- Per-op guesstimate (no long film, or when it cannot be trusted): use a default of 5 deg (6 for a typical adult in many systems) and adjust by context - short stature / wide pelvis / coxa vara -> larger angle (6-7); tall/ long femur or coxa valga -> smaller (4-5). Femoral bowing, prior fracture/ malunion or hip deformity make the IM-rod method unreliable: use extramedullary alignment, navigation/ robotics or a measured pre-op angle.
- Check after the cut with an alignment rod through the slot (should point to the femoral head centre) and verify with fluoroscopy or navigation if in doubt; remember the stem entry point and canal fill can shift the rod.

- Long-leg film is needed always (speaker); especially short or very tall patients. Devarsh also asked: look for more indications.
  Added (my list; verify vs speaker): a measured VCA matters most when the default 5-6 deg is likely wrong -
  (1) short stature or very tall patients (femoral length changes the anatomical-mechanical angle);
  (2) femoral bowing (usually lateral/ anterolateral; common in Indian/ Asian knees) or any extra-articular deformity - old fracture/ malunion, osteotomy, rickets, Paget, fibrous dysplasia;
  (3) hip pathology - coxa vara/ valga, dysplasia, previous THA or proximal femoral hardware;
  (4) severe varus or valgus deformity (>~15 deg) and fixed flexion deformity (a flexed knee distorts the measured angle; get the film with the knee as extended as possible);
  (5) IM canal obstruction or distal femoral hardware where an IM guide cannot be used;
  (6) wide pelvis (usually women) and neuromuscular or post-polio limbs;
  (7) revision or complex primary, and whenever computer navigation/ robotic or patient-specific alignment is not available as a cross-check.
  Technique of film: standing, weight-bearing, patellae forward (correct for rotation), both limbs in the same film; rotation errors and flexed knees distort angles. A standard knee AP film cannot see the hip centre so it cannot give a reliable mechanical axis.

## Slide: Effect of the valgus cut angle setting (diagram)
- Left: IM rod in the femoral canal with distal cut lines set at 3, 6 and 9 deg valgus; red line = mechanical axis; label "knee angle is 3 degrees relative to mechanical axis".
- Right: femur with the same 3 / 6 / 9 deg cut lines drawn against the anatomical axis, with the mechanical axis as the vertical reference.
**Added (my reading of the diagram):**
- If this femur's true anatomical-mechanical angle is ~6 deg, a 6 deg setting gives a cut perpendicular to the mechanical axis (neutral). 3 deg leaves the cut in ~3 deg of varus to the mechanical axis (femoral component varus); 9 deg puts it in ~3 deg of valgus. Each degree of error in the setting moves the cut by about the same degree.
- "Knee angle is 3 deg relative to the mechanical axis" = the native distal femoral joint line sits ~3 deg varus to the mechanical axis (mLDFA ~87). A perpendicular (mechanical) cut therefore restores ~3 deg more valgus than native; this is the difference that later "kinematic/ personalised alignment" talks about (recreating the native 3 deg).
- Take-home: the guide setting must match the patient's own angle, hence the long-leg film, rather than a fixed number.

## Slide: IM entry point for the distal femoral cut
- Photo of distal femur (trochlea/ notch) with dots: green = correct entry point (just above the intercondylar notch), red = wrong, one on each side.
- Left text: "The entry point hole is too medial" -> varus cut. Right text (cut off in the photo): "entry point hole is too lateral" -> valgus cut.
**Added:**
- Entry point sits at the roof of the notch, slightly medial to the centre of the trochlear groove and just anterior to the PCL origin (~1 cm), in line with the femoral canal. Medial entry tilts the rod/ guide so the cut ends up in varus; lateral entry gives valgus - one reason the long-leg film angle alone is not enough.
- Technique tips: drill 8-9 mm hole in line with the shaft axis; pass the rod slowly and check it slides freely - a bowed canal or a rod resting on cortex gives false alignment; clear fat/ debris and consider venting/ suction to lower fat-embolism risk (bilateral TKR especially); a rod that fits snugly (canal filling) is more accurate than a thin rod floating in a wide canal.
- Cross-check: after the cut, rod through the slot / alignment guide should point to the femoral head; if it does not, recheck rather than accept.

## Slide: Resection thickness = implant thickness (9 mm example)
- Left: cutting block on the femur showing 9 mm bone resected at each of the distal and posterior condyle (and anterior) cuts.
- Right: femoral component outline over the cut femur (lateral view, rotated): 9 mm distal cut = 9 mm of metal, 9 mm posterior cut = 9 mm of metal; "Joint line unchanged". Speaker's red marks emphasise equal thickness at distal and posterior.
**Added:**
- Principle: resect the same thickness of bone as the metal that replaces it (typical implant ~9-10 mm distal/ posterior; confirm with the system's specs), so the joint line and gap stay as they were, after allowing for cartilage and bone wear (worn condyle: cut less from that side or measure from the unworn condyle and add wear back).
- Check the posterior cut thickness with a caliper on the cut bone/ resected piece (and subtract the cartilage); a thin resected piece means the component will sit proud and tighten the flexion gap, a thick one loosens it.
- Cut less than the metal thickness -> tight, joint line distal/ lowered; cut more -> loose, joint line raised. Anterior cut should also be flush to avoid notching (anterior femoral cortex).
- Linked to the distal femur cut slide: the cut thickness decides the extension gap, the posterior thickness decides the flexion gap.

## Slide: Posterior referencing (femoral sizing / 4-in-1 block position)
- Lateral view: sizing guide with feet hooked on the posterior condyles and the stylus on the anterior femoral cortex (stylus scale marked 5, 3, 2); red lines show the posterior cut 9 mm (equal to the 9 mm of metal) and "9.5" written on the femur; the speaker added handwritten 9 / 12 marks near the posterior condyle; joint line unchanged.
**Added:**
- Posterior referencing: the guide sits on the posterior condyles so the posterior cut is always the implant thickness (e.g. 9 mm) - posterior condylar offset and the flexion gap stay as planned. Size error shows up at the front: a smaller size cuts deeper at the anterior cortex (risk of notching, and weaker bone = periprosthetic fracture risk), a larger size leaves the anterior flange proud (overstuffed patellofemoral joint, stiffness/ pain).
- Between sizes: with posterior referencing, going down a size risks notching, going up overstuffs the patella; many surgeons choose the larger size only if the AP/ML width allows and check the anterior flange does not overhang medio-laterally, or downsize and shift the guide anteriorly (using the stylus scale) to avoid a notch.
- Contrast with anterior referencing: cortex is protected (no notch) but a size error appears in the flexion gap (smaller size = looser flexion gap, larger = tighter).
- Checks after the cut: anterior cut flush with the cortex (no notch >3 mm), posterior thickness ~ equal to the metal, then proceed to flexion gap balance. (Reading of the handwriting is uncertain; confirm with the speaker.)

- Posterior referencing: notching and overstuffing (Devarsh asked for the problems).
  Added: NOTCHING (anterior cortex breached by the anterior cut, typically with an undersized femur or the guide set too far back). Matters because it weakens the supracondylar femur (cadaveric/ biomechanical work shows reduced torsional and bending strength); clinical periprosthetic fracture risk is mainly with deeper notches and osteoporotic bone (older women, RA, steroids). Prevent: check the stylus/ feeler gauge before cutting, size correctly, shift the guide anteriorly or move to the next size when between sizes, confirm with the cutting block that the anterior cut sits flush. If a notch happens, assess depth; deeper notches may need protected weight-bearing, avoid a stemmed/ more constrained decision in haste, consider stem extension or bone grafting per local practice.
  OVERSTUFFING (oversized femur, anterior flange proud): raises anterior condylar offset and patellofemoral contact forces -> anterior knee pain, reduced flexion, stiffness, patellar maltracking. An oversized component can also overhang medio-laterally and irritate soft tissue (ML overhang is associated with pain). Prevent: avoid upsizing just because the posterior referencing size is between; prefer sizing down plus anterior shift if the AP/ ML fit allows; use narrow/ gender-specific femurs when ML width is smaller than AP; check patellar tracking and patellar composite thickness (do not leave the patella thicker than native).
  Trade-off: shifting a downsized femur anteriorly enlarges the flexion gap, so check the flexion gap with the spacer and balance it against the extension gap (e.g. a thicker insert if both are loose).

- Anterior referencing (Devarsh, speaker): flexion gap increases, i.e. loose in flexion (when the femur is downsized/ between sizes).
  Added: The guide is set off the anterior cortex, so the anterior cut is always flush (no notching, no overstuffing). Size is read from the posterior condyles; a smaller size than the true AP width moves the posterior cut anteriorly, removing more posterior bone than the implant replaces -> flexion gap bigger (loose in flexion) and posterior condylar offset reduced (earlier posterior impingement/ less deep flexion; PS cam-post jump risk if very loose). A larger size does the opposite: less posterior bone removed -> tight flexion gap, increased posterior offset.
  Fixes for a loose flexion gap: upsize the femur (if ML width allows), use posterior femoral augments, or fill the gap with a thicker tibial insert and rebalance extension (distal femoral augments or taking a bit more distal bone/ avoid raising the joint line); ensure PCL/ posterior structures are not lax.
  Summary: Posterior referencing = size error shows at the front (notch or overstuff); Anterior referencing = size error shows in the flexion gap (smaller = loose, larger = tight).

## Slide: CR vs PS femoral component (lateral X-rays + implant photos)
- Implant photos: CR femur has an open notch (pegs only); PS femur has a closed box with a cam bar (red arrows), and the PS tibial insert has a post.
- CR-TKA X-ray: speaker's note "no box, only pegs to help with fixation"; the PCL (pink) is preserved.
- PS-TKA X-ray: "box in the femur component to make room for the POST (part of poly)"; yellow outlines the post/ insert, blue outlines the box/ femoral component.
**Added:**
- In PS the post engages the femoral cam in flexion, driving rollback in place of the PCL (usually from ~60-70 deg of flexion), so a box cut (intercondylar bone resection) is needed: this removes more distal-posterior femoral bone and slightly weakens the femur (supracondylar fracture risk), unlike CR where only peg holes are drilled.
- Because the PCL is excised in PS, the flexion gap is usually larger than in CR and balancing is easier; in CR the PCL must be functional and appropriately tensioned.
- Check on lateral X-ray: PS - box and post position, cam engaged at flexion; CR - PCL tension via tibial slope and rollback.


### Posterior vs anterior referencing in TKA (consolidated explanation)
- Sizing guide decides femoral AP size and where the anterior and posterior cuts sit.
- POSTERIOR referencing: guide rests on posterior condyles; stylus on anterior cortex reads size. Posterior cut thickness = implant posterior thickness (~9 mm), so posterior condylar offset and flexion gap are preserved. Size error shows ANTERIORLY: between sizes, round down = anterior notching risk; round up = overstuffed PFJ (stiff flexion, patellar overload). Pro: predictable flexion gap. Con: notching/overstuffing.
- ANTERIOR referencing: guide/stylus on anterior cortex; anterior cut fixed (no notching, flange flush). Size error shows POSTERIORLY in the flexion gap: undersize = more posterior bone cut = loose flexion gap; oversize = less posterior cut = tight flexion gap. Pro: no notching, PFJ predictable. Con: flexion gap unpredictable.
- Between sizes: posterior ref, choose by anterior cortex and PFJ; consider half-sizes or AP-adjustable systems. Anterior ref, check flexion gap with trial; adjust insert/size.
- Gap-balancing technique makes femoral rotation and AP position follow the flexion gap, so referencing matters less.
- Memory hook: Posterior ref = flexion gap safe, front pays. Anterior ref = front safe, flexion gap pays.
