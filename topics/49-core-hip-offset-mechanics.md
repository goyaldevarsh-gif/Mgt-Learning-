### Cantilever mechanism in the THA femoral stem [standard text (Gruen modes of stem failure); confirm against speaker's slide]
- Biomechanics: the joint reaction force (about 3-4 x body weight in gait) acts on the head, medial to the stem axis (offset = lever arm). Bending moment = force x offset, so the stem behaves like a cantilever beam fixed distally: lateral surface in TENSION, medial surface in COMPRESSION.
- Normally the proximal medial bone/calcar and the cement/bone support share this load. When proximal support is lost (calcar resorption, proximal loosening, bone loss) while the distal stem is still firmly fixed, the whole bending moment goes through the stem: the stem works as a cantilever and can fail by fatigue fracture (usually at the junction of the loose proximal and well-fixed distal stem, mid-stem).
- Gruen 1979 modes of failure of cemented stems: pistoning (stem subsides in cement), medial midstem pivot, calcar pivot, and cantilever bending (distal fixation, proximal loss of support).
- Risk factors: heavy/obese or active patient, high offset or long neck (longer lever arm), thin/small-diameter stem, varus-placed stem, poor proximal support (undersized stem, poor cement/bone, osteoporosis), proximal loosening with distal fixation (e.g. extensively coated stems with proximal osteolysis), modular junctions, weaker alloy or manufacturing defects.
- Link to stress shielding: distal fixation unloads the proximal femur and keeps loading the stem as a cantilever.
- Prevention: ensure proximal/calcar support and canal fill, avoid varus, adequate stem diameter for the patient, correct offset (restore rather than exaggerate), adequate cement mantle, strong materials/design (forged, thicker proximal section), monitor for proximal loosening on follow-up.

**Cantilever failure in plain words (explanation for revision):** think of a diving board. A board clamped near the end where you stand barely bends; clamped far away, the same weight bends it much more. The stem is the board, the "clamp" is where the stem is firmly held by bone/cement, the load is the head force. Bending moment = force x distance from the head to the point where the stem is firmly held. Normally the proximal femur grips the stem near the head (short distance, small moment). If the proximal bone/cement stops supporting it (calcar resorption, proximal loosening), the first firm grip moves down the stem (long distance), so the same body weight gives a much bigger moment, maximal at the transition from loose to well-fixed stem. Every step (about a million cycles a year) bends the stem: tension on the lateral side opens a fatigue crack there, which grows until the stem breaks. Illustration only: lever 5 cm -> 15 cm = 3x the bending moment.

### Intra-operative checks in THA: leg length (vertical offset), femoral/global offset (horizontal), shuck test, stability [standard text; numbers approximate; confirm against speaker's slides]
**1. Leg length (vertical) ways:**
- Pre-op template as the plan: neck cut level above lesser trochanter, planned change in LLD; marks on the femur as reference.
- Fixed-pin caliper method: pin/Steinmann pin in the ilium (or supra-acetabular) and a mark on the greater trochanter/femur; measure the distance before dislocation and after the final reduction with a caliper (changes show the lengthening/shortening). Compare with the plan.
- Compare the limbs: legs together in extension, compare medial malleoli/heels and knees (knee-to-knee, foot-to-foot) against the opposite side (watch for pelvic obliquity and pre-op structural LLD).
- Palpate the tip of the greater trochanter relative to the centre of the femoral head (trochanter tip about at head centre level).
- Soft-tissue tension (shuck test below), and trial neck/head lengths (+0/+3.5/+7 etc.).
- Fluoroscopy/X-ray, navigation or robotics for objective measurement.
**2. Femoral/global offset (horizontal):**
- Offset = distance from the femoral axis to the centre of the head (femoral offset) plus the acetabular medialisation (global offset).
- Compare with the contralateral side and with the template; choose standard vs high-offset stem or neck/liner; avoid over-medialising the cup (reaming) and avoid excessive neck length instead of offset.
- Soft-tissue check: abductor tension (greater trochanter to ilium distance), palpate gluteal tension; a good offset restores abductor lever arm and reduces impingement; too little offset = lax abductors, limp, instability; too much = tight, trochanteric pain.
- Imaging check (fluoroscopy/AP) of offset against the template.
**3. Shuck test (telescoping/pistoning):** after the trial (or final) reduction, with the hip in extension/neutral, apply longitudinal traction to the limb and look for the head sliding out of the cup. A small shuck (a few mm) is expected; none = too tight (likely lengthened or too much offset); excessive = lax (too short/low offset). Use it together with the leg-length and stability tests; adjust the neck length or offset.
**4. Stability and impingement tests with trials:** flexion to about 90 deg with internal rotation (posterior stability), extension with external rotation (anterior stability), and abduction/adduction; look for bony or prosthetic impingement, levering out; confirm no excess motion or edge loading; check head-neck ratio. Re-check after final components.
**5. LLD guide numbers:** lengthening or shortening of about 10 mm or less is usually tolerated; more is symptomatic (limp, back pain); lengthening of more than about 3-4 cm raises sciatic/peroneal nerve palsy risk (stretch). Rule of thumb: do not accept lengthening to gain stability without checking the nerve and the plan.

### Effect of restoration of femoral offset (slide)
**Slide:** Moves the femur laterally: improves soft tissue tension; decreases impingement; better stability without lengthening the leg. Decreases the force the abductor muscles require: improves gait; resultant (joint reaction) force decreases, resulting in less wear and loosening. (Image: AP hip with femoral offset marked; lines from the femoral shaft axis to the head centre.)
**Added (explanation):**
- Femoral offset = perpendicular distance from the femoral shaft axis to the centre of the femoral head. More offset moves the femur laterally and the abductor insertion (greater trochanter) away from the pelvis, lengthening the abductor lever arm.
- Biomechanics: abductor force x its lever arm must balance body weight x its lever arm (single-leg stance). A longer abductor lever arm means less abductor force is needed, so the joint reaction force (abductor force + body weight) falls: less polyethylene wear and less load on the cup/stem interface (loosening).
- Soft-tissue tension and stability: restoring offset tightens abductors and the capsule without lengthening the limb, which improves stability; the greater trochanter also moves away from the pelvic rim, reducing bony/prosthetic impingement.
- Practical: use high-offset/lateralised stem or neck instead of a longer neck/head to gain tension; restore (do not exaggerate) offset; too much offset overloads the stem (cantilever bending) and may cause trochanteric pain.
- Link: ties to the intra-op offset check, cantilever bending, and templating notes.

### How to increase femoral / global offset in the OT (THA) [standard text; confirm against speaker's slide]
Goal: restore native offset (aim within about 5 mm of the contralateral/templated value) WITHOUT lengthening the leg.
| Method | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| High-offset (lateralised) stem, i.e. lower neck-shaft angle (e.g. 127-132 deg vs 135) or stem offset options (e.g. Exeter 35.5/37.5/44/50) | Increases femoral offset with little change in leg length | First choice; offset stems have a bigger proximal bending moment (cantilever) |
| Offset neck / modular neck options (varus/anteverted/offset necks) | Adjusts offset and length separately | Modular junction: fretting/corrosion risk |
| Longer head/neck (+3.5, +7 mm) | Increases offset but also leg length (length rises more than offset) | Do not use to gain offset if the leg is already long |
| Lateralised/offset liner (offset-bore or lateralised acetabular liner) | Increases global offset (moves head centre laterally) | Shifts the cup lateral relative to the stem; impingement/edge loading |
| Do not over-medialise the cup / keep the COR at the native hip centre | Preserves global offset | Medial wall reaming loses offset; too lateral cup = overstuffing |
| Stem seating depth | Controls leg length (stem deeper = shorter, higher = longer); offset mostly unchanged for a given neck angle | Do not depend on depth to fix offset |
| Neck cut level + lateral stem position (avoid medialised/varus-valgus errors) | Valgus/neutral seating keeps offset; varus seating increases offset but shortens the leg | Excess varus = shortening, loosening risk |
| Greater trochanter procedures (trochanteric advancement/ distal-lateral transfer) in revision or abductor weakness | Improves abductor lever arm/tension | Only in selected revision cases |
**Pearls:** choose offset first (stem/liner design), then length (neck/head and depth); check with the shuck test and the pin/caliper leg-length method; too much offset = trochanteric pain, stem overload, tight abductors; too little = laxity, impingement, instability, limp.

### Coxa vara and coxa valga in THA: intra-op approach, offset and leg length [standard text; confirm against speaker's slides]
Normal neck-shaft angle (NSA) about 125-135 deg; coxa vara <~120-125 deg, coxa valga >~140 deg. Template on a true AP with the legs internally rotated ~15 deg (so the real NSA and offset are seen); use the contralateral hip if normal; check anteversion (valga is often anteverted, e.g. DDH).
| | Coxa vara | Coxa valga |
|---|---|---|
| Native anatomy | Short neck, head centre high, HIGH native offset, greater trochanter high/lateral (can impinge on the ilium), leg often short | Long neck, low native offset, greater trochanter lower, leg often long, abductor lever arm small |
| Main problems | A standard 135 deg stem loses offset (medialises the femur); stem tends to go into varus; difficult lateral canal access (overhanging GT); limb shortening and instability if offset lost | Standard stem lengthens the leg or tends to valgus; low offset = weak abductors, instability; anteversion may be excessive (cementless stems follow the canal) |
| Stem choice | High-offset/lateralised stem (lower NSA, e.g. 127-132 deg); consider modular neck; cemented stems with offset options (e.g. Exeter offset ladders) | Standard or even low-offset stem; if abductor lever arm is weak, restore normal offset with a high-offset stem, but do not lengthen the leg; if anteversion is high, use cemented/ modular stems to control version |
| Entry/reaming | Lateral entry (remove lateral GT bone/ "shoulder"), start reaming lateral to avoid varus; use a trochanteric reamer | Start more medial; avoid valgus/undersizing; beware of a posterior-lateral perforation |
| Neck cut | CUT MORE (lower cut): a standard stem would put the head centre too high, so the stem must sit deeper; check cut height from the lesser trochanter against the template | CUT LESS (higher cut): a standard stem would put the head centre too low (leg shortened), so the stem must sit higher; use the template (corrected on 21 Nov, see geometry note below) |
| Offset plan | Restore high offset (do not accept loss); watch trochanteric/ilial impingement | Aim for normal offset; avoid exaggeration; avoid long neck instead of offset |
| Intra-op checks | Trials: leg length (caliper/pin), shuck test, stability (flexion-IR, extension-ER), impingement of GT/pelvis, check no varus malposition on fluoroscopy | Same; extra care for lengthening (nerve risk if >3-4 cm), instability from low offset |
**Severe deformity** (post-SCFE, Perthes, malunion, DDH): consider a modular stem, subtrochanteric (derotation/shortening) osteotomy, or computer/ robotic planning; special cemented or custom stems.
**Remember:** goal = restore the native (or contralateral) COR, offset and leg length; choose offset by stem/liner design, length by neck/head and stem depth; always confirm with trials, shuck test and stability tests.

### Role of the femoral neck cut in THA (standard stems) [standard text; confirm against speaker's slides]
- The neck cut (osteotomy) decides where the stem/broach sits along the femur, so it sets LEG LENGTH and, with the neck/head choice, OFFSET. Templated as the distance above the lesser trochanter (and from the tip of the greater trochanter/head centre), typical angle about 45 deg to the shaft axis (cut angle/level per stem design).
- Rule: for a given stem and neck/head, higher cut = longer leg (stem sits higher); lower cut = shorter leg. Neck cut level mostly changes LENGTH; offset is controlled by stem design (NSA, offset option) and by head/neck choice.
- Standard stems (e.g. 135 deg): cut level is mostly a length decision. To gain offset you add a longer neck/head (offset rises but length rises more) or switch to a high-offset stem; a lower cut + longer neck restores length and gains offset.
| Cut | Effect | Problems / fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too high | Stem seats high, leg lengthened, impingement from remaining neck stub, tight soft tissues | Re-cut; shorter neck |
| Too low | Stem sits deep, leg shortened, laxity, loss of calcar support, may need a larger neck/head or high-offset stem | Use longer neck/head (watch offset vs length), augment with offset stem |
| Correct (per template) | Restores length; head centre at the planned height | Confirm with the trial, shuck test, pin/caliper and stability tests |
- Cementless wedge/taper stems: the cut also decides the final fit (stem should seat with the broach flush at the cut and calcar contact); cemented taper-slip (collarless) stems allow depth adjustment, so the length is set by cut + depth + head.
- Pearl: cut slightly HIGH and re-cut if needed (bone removal is irreversible); do not cut to "fit" the stem at the cost of length; use the lesser trochanter as the reference and check against the template.

**Why coxa vara = cut more, coxa valga = cut less (standard stem; geometry) [correction of my earlier valga neck-cut row]:**
- Head-centre position above the neck base depends on the neck-shaft angle (NSA). For a neck of length L: height along the shaft = L x cos(180-NSA), offset = L x sin(180-NSA). NSA 120 deg: height 0.50 L, offset 0.87 L. NSA 135 deg: height 0.71 L, offset 0.71 L. NSA 145 deg: height 0.82 L, offset 0.57 L.
- Coxa vara (low NSA): native head centre is LOW and offset is HIGH. A standard 135 deg stem puts the head higher and medially (leg lengthened, offset lost). To bring the head centre down (match the native/plan), the stem has to sit deeper, so the neck is cut MORE (lower). Use a high-offset/lateralised stem to recover the offset.
- Coxa valga (high NSA): native head centre is HIGH and offset LOW. A standard stem puts the head lower and more lateral (leg shortened). To keep the length, the stem has to sit higher, so cut LESS (higher cut), with a longer neck/head if needed.
- What can go wrong: wrong cut in vara = lengthening and loss of offset (instability); wrong cut in valga = shortening/laxity. Always confirm with the template, trials, pin/caliper and shuck test.
- Assumption: "jyada/less" read as how much neck is cut (level), confirm with speaker.
