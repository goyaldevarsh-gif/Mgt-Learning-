## Talk 4: Surgical Approaches in Primary TKA - Before the incision: previous skin incisions (slide)
- Multiple vertical scars: skin supply runs medial to lateral, so use the lateral-most incision to avoid lateral flap necrosis (X on medial scar, tick on lateral).
- New incision: keep a skin bridge of at least 5 cm from any old scar.
- Horizontal scar: cross it at a right angle, or at least at 60 deg.
**Added:**
- Anterior knee skin gets its blood supply mainly from the medial side (saphenous/ descending genicular perforators through the fascia) and the flap above the deep fascia is fragile; the old scar has already cut some vessels, so the new flap should keep the lateral-most scar side to preserve the remaining medial-to-lateral supply. Keep the incision full thickness down to fascia, avoid undermining skin and subcutaneous flaps.
- Prior incisions: if a midline scar exists and is usable, reuse it. Old arthroscopy portals or small transverse scars can usually be ignored. Very wide or adherent scars, prior skin grafts/ flaps, radiation or vascular disease: plastic surgery review, +/- skin-expander or delayed approach.
- Wound problems cause the worst complication - skin necrosis exposing the prosthesis leads to PJI; also check skin quality, diabetes, smoking, and consider tourniquet effect on perfusion in poor skin.

## Slide: Raising skin flaps (Before the incision)
- Too superficial: a plane in the fat divides the branches running up to the skin (marked wrong).
- Correct: deep to the muscular fascia; the flap carries its fascial plexus with it (marked right).
- Anterior knee skin is supplied by perforating arteries that run in the muscular fascia. Medial and lateral flaps, if needed, should be raised deep to this fascia to avoid skin necrosis.
**Added:**
- This is the fasciocutaneous blood supply: perforators from the deep vessels pierce the muscular fascia and form a subfascial/ suprafascial plexus that feeds the skin through the fat. Dissecting in fat strips that plexus off, leaving a thin skin/ fat flap with a random supply that is prone to ischaemia, especially in a scarred, diabetic or smoker's knee.
- Practical: after the skin incision go straight down to the extensor fascia/ retinaculum in one layer, then raise medial/ lateral flaps off the fascia (sharp, no-touch, minimal retraction). Keep flaps thick, avoid long thin flaps and forceful retraction or pressure under the tourniquet.

## Slide: Exposure sequence (Fig 2.1b - anteromedial parapatellar approach)
1. Incise the lateral patellofemoral plicae with the knee in extension, for better patellar mobility and easier eversion.
2. Evert or sublux the patella in extension, then flex the knee.
3. Elevate the anteromedial capsule and the deep MCL subperiosteally to the posteromedial corner.
4. Excise the infrapatellar fat pad.
5. If the patellar tendon is at risk of avulsion, fix it at the tibial tuberosity with a headed pin.
6. With the knee flexed, excise both menisci and the ACL; sacrifice or spare the PCL for a cruciate-sacrificing or cruciate-retaining TKR.
**Added:**
- Medial parapatellar (anteromedial) approach: the workhorse; capsule cut ~medial to the patella and quadriceps tendon, leaving a cuff for repair. Gives wide exposure, extensile, but disrupts the extensor mechanism and the patellar blood supply (superior/ inferior lateral geniculates are the main supply once medial ones are cut).
- Step 1 matters: releasing the lateral plica/ adhesions relieves tension on the patellar tendon insertion so eversion does not avulse it. Keep the knee in extension while everting, flex gradually and do not force.
- Step 5 rescue: if the tendon starts to peel off the tubercle, stop and secure it (headed pin or suture anchor/ staple) or perform a quad snip / V-Y or tibial tubercle osteotomy for a stiff knee.
- Step 3: subperiosteal release preserves the MCL and is the first part of medial balancing in a varus knee. Fat-pad excision aids exposure but some prefer partial resection to protect patellar blood supply and tendon.
- Alternatives: midvastus, subvastus, lateral parapatellar (valgus knee) - the differences in long-term outcome are small; surgeon familiarity and knee pathology decide.

## Slide: Patellar blood supply (anteromedial approach)
- The patella is fed by a ring of genicular vessels; the medial parapatellar approach puts this supply at risk, especially when a lateral release is needed.
  1. Medial arthrotomy divides the medial supply.
  2. Fat-pad excision removes the inferior supply.
  3. Lateral release can divide the superior lateral genicular artery (SLGA).
- The SLGA may be the last blood supply after medial parapatellar arthrotomy and fat-pad excision.
- Key: DGA descending genicular; SMGA/IMGA superior/inferior medial genicular; SLGA/ILGA superior/inferior lateral genicular; ATRA anterior tibial recurrent.
**Added:**
- Anastomotic ring around the patella plus an intraosseous supply (mid-patellar vessels via the anterior surface, polar vessels). Compromise raises risk of avascular necrosis/ patellar fracture, especially with resurfacing (reaming/ cutting further reduces the intraosseous supply).
- Protective habits: preserve the SLGA, avoid an unnecessary lateral release (use "pie-crusting"/ check tracking first, correct component rotation and alignment, which usually removes the need), preserve at least part of the fat pad when possible, avoid stripping the patella.
- If a lateral release is needed, perform it from inside-out ~1 cm from the patellar edge, leaving the SLGA intact where possible (it runs along the superolateral border).
- Evidence from bone-scan/ cadaver studies: a medial parapatellar approach plus lateral release leaves the patella with markedly reduced perfusion; clinical significance is modest, but patellar fracture/ AVN risk rises with resurfacing plus lateral release.

### Subvastus approach (Hofmann, "Southern")
**Slide:** Vastus medialis lifted off the intermuscular septum; its insertion on the extensor mechanism stays intact. AVOID IN: obese, bulky vastus medialis, short femur, patella baja, previous HTO, previous medial parapatellar arthrotomy. Benefits: early rehab, better PF tracking, less need for lateral release, improved post-op flexion (quad release). Limitations: technically difficult, patella subluxed not everted, harder in obese/muscular.
**Added:** Quadriceps tendon is not cut, so extensor mechanism and (standard text) the superomedial geniculate supply to the patella are better preserved than in medial parapatellar. Meta-analyses mostly show less early pain / faster straight-leg raise, but no clear long-term difference; some report longer operative time. Patella baja and previous HTO are avoided because exposure without eversion/quad release is already limited.

### Subvastus technique
**Slide:** Midline incision ~5 cm above the superior pole of patella to just medial to tibial tuberosity. Incise deep fascia over quadriceps tendon in midline; palpate proximally, release if tight. Expose the inferior border of vastus medialis. Lift the muscle off the intermuscular septum by blunt dissection ~10 cm proximal to the adductor tubercle. Inverted-L capsulotomy: vertical limb along medial border of patellar tendon, horizontal limb along inferior border of VM. Divide anterior horn of medial meniscus; medial subperiosteal release as routine.
**Added:** Watch for the descending genicular / saphenous branches near the adductor tubercle region, and haematoma under the VMO (slide's table lists haematoma and overstretching of VMO fibres as specific risks). Subluxate (do not evert) the patella and flex the knee to deliver the tibia.

### Midvastus approach (Engh)
**Slide:** Popularised by Engh to preserve quadriceps and extensor mechanism; VM insertion on the quadriceps tendon not damaged. Advantages: early rehab, improved PF tracking, less lateral release. Relatively contraindicated in: obesity, flexion deformity, pre-op flexion <80 deg, previous HTO, hypertrophic arthritis. Specific risk: denervation of VMO fibres.
**Added:** The muscle is split in line of its fibres from the superomedial patellar pole; keeping the split limited (standard text: about 4 cm) lowers the risk of denervating VMO fibres. Stiff knees (flexion <80 deg) and hypertrophic arthritis need more exposure than this approach comfortably gives.

### Comparing the three medial approaches (table)
| | Medial parapatellar | Midvastus | Subvastus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Route | Through quadriceps tendon | Through vastus medialis, along its fibres | Under vastus medialis, off the septum |
| VMO insertion | Divided | Preserved | Preserved |
| Exposure | Utilitarian; most commonly used | Adequate | Adequate; difficult in obese and muscular |
| Lateral release | Needed more often | Less often | Less often |
| Early recovery | Standard | Less blood loss and pain; faster quad recovery | Less blood loss and pain; faster quad recovery |
| Specific risks | Maltracking; patellar blood supply; infrapatellar nerve | Denervation of VMO fibres | Haematoma below VMO; overstretching of VMO fibres |
**Added:** "All three provide adequate exposure." Evidence favours at most small early benefits for the muscle-sparing ones; choose by patient factors (BMI, stiffness, deformity, prior surgery) and surgeon familiarity. Medial parapatellar remains the default and the most extensile (quad snip, TTO possible).

### Anterolateral approach (for valgus knee beyond 20 deg)
**Slide:** For the valgus knee beyond 20 deg, with a coronal Z-plasty of the lateral retinaculum (photos courtesy Dr Satish BRJ).
**Technique slide:** Anterior midline incision; raise lateral skin flap. Coronal Z-plasty of the lateral retinaculum: superficial anteromedial layer and deep posterolateral layer. Include retropatellar fat pad and lateral meniscus in the lateral flap. Release the ITB from Gerdy's tubercle; extend into the anterior compartment fascia if needed. Superiorly incise rectus tendon along its lateral margin. Proximal lateral release as a sleeve, up to the middle of the lateral condyle. Difficult exposure: add a quadriceps snip running distal-lateral to proximal-medial.
**Added:** Known as the Keblish-type lateral approach; it releases the tight lateral structures as part of the exposure instead of through a medial approach plus piecemeal release. This is the "rigid valgus -> lateral approach" branch of the valgus algorithm slide (Day 1). Z-plasty also allows lengthened closure of the lateral retinaculum.

### Quadriceps snip
**Slide:** WHEN: difficult exposures when adequate flexion cannot be achieved with the usual approach and releases. HOW: oblique proximal extension of the parapatellar arthrotomy, distal-medial to proximal-lateral in the anteromedial approach; the reverse in the anterolateral approach. CLOSURE: simply an extension of the closure of the parapatellar arthrotomy. REHAB: no change.
**Added:** Least morbid of the extensile options (snip < V-Y turndown < tibial tubercle osteotomy). Reach for it early, before patellar tendon avulsion becomes the risk.
