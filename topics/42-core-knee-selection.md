## Talk 3: Patient Selection, Preoperative Evaluation and Planning
**Slide: First step - confirm the right indication**
- Concordant symptoms + examination + radiographic findings/disease (all three must agree). Example X-ray: bilateral hip AVN.
- Footer: 2023 ACR/AAHKS timing guideline - individualise timing and shared decision making.
**Added:**
- "Treat the patient, not the X-ray": a bad X-ray with mild symptoms is not an indication, and severe pain with a normal-looking X-ray needs another diagnosis.
- Mimics to exclude: hip-spine syndrome / lumbar radiculopathy (buttock-thigh pain, neuro signs), referred hip pain presenting as knee pain, trochanteric pain syndrome, vascular claudication, CRPS, inflammatory arthritis, neuropathic pain.
- Hip clues: groin pain, C-sign, painful/limited IR in flexion, FADIR. Knee clues: activity/weight-bearing pain, joint-line tenderness, localised to knee, matching compartment on weight-bearing films.
- Imaging: weight-bearing films (Rosenberg PA flexion view in knee; AP pelvis + lateral in hip) and grade (Kellgren-Lawrence). For AVN use Ficat/ARCO stage; collapse (crescent sign) and acetabular involvement push toward THA.
- Pre-conditions before elective surgery: failure of adequate non-operative care (weight loss, exercise/physio, analgesics, injections as appropriate), then optimise modifiable risks (anaemia, diabetes control HbA1c, BMI, smoking, nutrition, active infection, dental/ skin/ urine foci). Exact thresholds are individualised per the guideline; check the guideline text before quoting cut-offs.

- 3 indications of TKR (Devarsh): painful knee, unstable knee, decreased ROM (functional limitation).
  Added: Pain is the main indication (night/rest pain, walking limitation) with radiographic arthritis and failed non-op care. Instability and ROM loss alone, without pain/arthritis, are weak indications. Common underlying causes: primary OA (most), inflammatory arthritis (RA), post-traumatic, osteonecrosis, haemophilic arthropathy. Fixed flexion deformity >~15-20 deg or gross deformity (varus/valgus) is a stronger reason to operate since it worsens gait and loads. Contraindications: active infection, extensor mechanism disruption, severe vascular disease, neuropathic (Charcot) joint (relative), unrealistic expectations or medical unfitness.

- Dynamic stiffness in knee OA (Devarsh: link to knee OA).
  Added (interpretation, verify vs speaker): In knee OA stiffness is typically start-up / gelling stiffness after rest or sitting, lasting <30 min and easing with movement (vs inflammatory arthritis >60 min morning stiffness). Fixed (static) stiffness reflects mechanical loss of ROM: osteophytes, capsular/ hamstring contracture, effusion, and fixed flexion deformity. Dynamic component (pain-guarding, quadriceps inhibition, effusion) is reversible and improves with warm-up, physio, and under anaesthesia - which matters in planning: dynamic stiffness is largely treatable, fixed stiffness predicts post-TKA ROM (preop flexion is the strongest predictor of postop flexion).

- Instability check (Devarsh): watch gait for 15-20 steps with the knee exposed.
  Added: Look for varus/valgus thrust (lateral thrust at heel strike = dynamic collateral laxity or medial OA), recurvatum/ back-knee thrust (quadriceps weakness, posterior capsule laxity), antalgic or stiff-knee gait, giving-way/buckling. Follow with stress tests: varus/valgus at 0 and 30 deg, Lachman, posterior drawer, sag sign. Why it matters: preop ligament status guides the constraint choice (CR/PS vs CCK) and release plan. Varus thrust is linked to faster medial OA progression (Chang 2004).

- How to check dynamic stiffness (Devarsh asked).
  Added (my method; verify vs speaker): (1) Active vs passive ROM with goniometer - passive > active suggests quadriceps inhibition/ pain-guarding (dynamic); both equally limited suggests fixed/mechanical. (2) Re-check after warm-up (heel slides, few cycles) or gentle sustained stretch - improvement = dynamic. (3) End-feel: soft/ springy or spasm = dynamic; hard/ bony block = osteophyte; firm capsular = contracture. (4) Extension: heel-prop test (supine, heel on a roll) for fixed flexion deformity; rule out hip flexion contracture (Thomas test) and hamstring tightness (popliteal angle). (5) Effusion (bulge/ patellar tap) and patellar mobility. (6) Gold standard for separating the two: exam under anaesthesia (ROM improves = dynamic).

## Slide: Red flags before consenting
- Pain out of proportion to imaging, or unexplained night pain.
- Active skin, dental or systemic infection; unexplained fever or wound.
- Severe untreated depression, catastrophising, chronic opioid dependence.
- Unrealistic expectation of a "normal" knee or unrestricted high-impact activity.
**Added:**
- Pain out of proportion / night pain: think PJI-like or other causes (infection, tumour, AVN/ insufficiency fracture, CRPS, referred hip/spine pain); investigate before offering TKA.
- Infection: elective surgery should wait until treated; active skin lesions near the incision (ulcer, eczema, tinea), UTI, dental sepsis. Unexplained fever/wound = rule out septic arthritis first.
- Psychological: depression, catastrophising and preop opioid use predict worse pain and satisfaction after TKA (roughly 15-20% of TKA patients remain dissatisfied; persistent pain in ~10-20%). Treat/ optimise first, counsel on expectations.
- Expectations: TKA gives a pain-free, functional knee, not a normal one. Typical advice: low-impact activities (walking, cycling, swimming, golf); avoid running/jumping sports. Document expectations in consent.

- Resting pain in knee OA: think something else and work up (Devarsh).
  Added: Differentials - low-grade/ septic arthritis, crystal arthropathy (gout, pseudogout), osteonecrosis (SPONK/ SONK), subchondral insufficiency or stress fracture, referred pain (hip OA, lumbar radiculopathy), tumour (e.g. osteoid osteoma), inflammatory arthritis, CRPS, neuropathic or vascular pain, pes anserine bursitis. Work-up - history (fever, weight loss, trauma, night pain, steroid use) and exam (warmth, effusion, hip ROM, spine, neuro, pulses); bloods: CBC, ESR, CRP, uric acid, RF/anti-CCP if indicated, vitamin D; X-ray knee (weight-bearing) +/- hip/spine; MRI (marrow oedema, SONK, stress fracture, tumour); aspiration if effusion or warm joint (cell count, crystals, culture); bone scan, NCS or Doppler if indicated.

## Slide: Risk stratification - do not use one number
- Diabetes, obesity, anaemia, renal dysfunction, malnutrition and smoking cluster.
- ASA class, frailty, cardiopulmonary disease and functional capacity matter.
- A threshold triggers optimisation and shared decision-making, not automatic denial.
- Document modifiable risks and the agreed plan.
**Added:**
- Common optimisation targets used in practice (individualise; check local protocol): HbA1c <7.5-8% (fructosamine as alternative), Hb >=13 g/dL in either sex for major surgery per PBM (earlier note said 12 for women; corrected per anaemia slide; treat iron deficiency with IV iron if oral fails), albumin >3.5 g/dL, eGFR check and renal-dose drugs, smoking cessation >=4-6 weeks, BMI: counsel on weight loss; BMI >40 raises infection, wound and revision risk.
- Frailty and function: simple tools (Clinical Frailty Scale, TUG, 4-m gait speed) predict complications, longer stay and discharge to facility better than age alone.
- ASA III-IV or poor cardiopulmonary reserve: anaesthesia/physician review before listing, not a refusal.
- Why "not one number": a single BMI or HbA1c cut-off excludes patients who would benefit most; the 2023 ACR/AAHKS guideline favours individualised optimisation. Also check MRSA/ S. aureus colonisation and decolonise if screening is available.

## Slide: HbA1c - what should we aim for?
- ADA 2026: aim for HbA1c <8% measured within 3 months before elective surgery.
- Many arthroplasty optimisation pathways use <7.5% as a pragmatic target.
- Interpret HbA1c cautiously with anaemia, haemoglobinopathies and CKD.
- Refs on slide: ADA Standards of Care 2026; ACR/AAHKS 2023; MacMahon et al. 2022.
**Added:**
- HbA1c reflects ~3 months of glycaemia, hence the 3-month window. It is unreliable when red-cell lifespan changes: iron-deficiency anaemia (falsely high), haemolysis/ recent transfusion/ blood loss (falsely low), haemoglobinopathies (HbS, HbC, thalassaemia - common in India), CKD/ ESA use.
- In those cases use fructosamine (~2-3 weeks), fasting glucose and perioperative glucose logs / CGM instead.
- Also optimise perioperative glucose (see next slide: ADA 2026 target is 100-180 mg/dL; earlier note of 140-180 corrected). Poor control raises PJI, wound complications, AKI and readmission; very tight control or an overly strict cut-off can delay surgery in patients who need it, hence the pragmatic 7.5-8% range.

## Slide: Newer glycaemic indices - beyond HbA1c
- Fructosamine reflects ~2-3 weeks; >=292-293 umol/L linked to higher arthroplasty complications.
- Glycated albumin reflects ~2-3 weeks; >15.5% reported as higher risk in recent reviews.
- These are risk markers; validate in context and avoid treating all cut-offs as universal.
- Refs on slide: 2026 systematic review of fructosamine; Khan et al., JAAOS Global 2026; ADA 2026.
**Added:**
- Both measure glycation of serum proteins (fructosamine = all glycated serum proteins, mostly albumin; glycated albumin = albumin fraction alone), so they are independent of red-cell lifespan - useful when HbA1c is unreliable (anaemia, haemoglobinopathy, CKD) and for a short-term view in a patient whose control is changing.
- Caveats: low albumin (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition), thyroid disease and obesity alter results (glycated albumin tends to read lower in obesity); assays not widely standardised, so cut-offs differ by lab/population. HbA1c remains the standard first test; use these as add-ons.

## Slide: Perioperative glucose - exact targets
- ADA 2026: 100-180 mg/dL before, during and after surgery.
- Persistent inpatient glucose >=180 mg/dL: initiate or intensify treatment.
- Avoid hypoglycaemia and excessive variability, not just high mean glucose.
- Check day-of-surgery capillary glucose and ensure a written insulin plan.
- Ref on slide: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes 2026, hospital/perioperative care.
**Added:**
- Hypoglycaemia (<70 mg/dL) and wide glucose swings are independently linked to worse outcomes (infection, AKI, mortality), so avoid over-aggressive insulin; mean glucose alone is a poor metric.
- Practical: capillary glucose on arrival and pre-induction; recheck every 1-2 h intra-op in longer cases and every 4-6 h post-op (more often on insulin infusion); basal-bolus (basal + correction) insulin rather than sliding scale alone. Oral agents: hold per drug (SGLT2 inhibitors stop ~3-4 days before for euglycaemic DKA risk; metformin per renal function); written plan for basal insulin dose reduction on the day.
- Steroids (e.g., dexamethasone for PONV) raise glucose for 12-24 h; plan for it.

## Slide: Diabetes drugs - preoperative action
- SGLT2 inhibitors: stop empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin >=3 days; ertugliflozin >=4 days.
- GLP-1 agonists: individualise aspiration risk with anaesthesia; do not apply blanket discontinuation.
- Metformin: continuation may be reasonable in selected patients; account for renal function and local anaesthesia protocol.
- Insulin: adjust basal/bolus regimen rather than omitting all insulin.
- Refs: 2026 AHA/ACC perioperative guideline; ADA 2026. Medication decisions require local protocol.
**Added:**
- SGLT2i: risk is euglycaemic DKA (glucose near normal, so easily missed); check ketones if unwell post-op, restart only when eating and drinking normally.
- GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide): delay gastric emptying, so retained gastric contents despite fasting. Higher-risk features: escalation phase/ recent dose increase, high dose, GI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, bloating), weekly agents. Options: liquid diet 24 h before, point-of-care gastric ultrasound, or full-stomach precautions (RSI) - decision made with anaesthetist. Stopping unnecessarily risks hyperglycaemia.
- Metformin: main concern is lactic acidosis with AKI/ contrast; commonly held on the morning of surgery or continued if eGFR is adequate (follow local protocol).
- Insulin: typically give ~75-80% of basal dose the night before/ morning of surgery (long-acting); omit prandial insulin while fasting; never stop basal insulin in type 1 (DKA risk). Sulfonylureas: hold on the day of surgery (hypoglycaemia). Make surgery first on the list for diabetic patients where possible.

## Slide: Anaemia - a modifiable complication risk
- Screen CBC ideally >=4 weeks preoperatively.
- Patient blood management (PBM) commonly defines preoperative anaemia as Hb <13 g/dL in either sex for major surgery.
- Investigate ferritin, transferrin saturation, CRP, B12, folate and kidney disease as indicated.
- Treat iron deficiency (oral or IV depending on urgency), not with routine transfusion.
- Ref: International Consensus Conference on Anemia Management in Surgical Patients; PBM principles.
**Added:**
- Why 4 weeks: time for iron to raise Hb (oral ~1-2 g/dL in 3-4 weeks; IV works in 1-2 weeks). Preop anaemia is linked to more transfusion, longer stay, infection, and higher mortality after arthroplasty.
- Iron deficiency: ferritin <30 ng/mL (or <100 with inflammation/ CKD) and TSAT <20%. Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, hence CRP and TSAT alongside.
- Oral iron: alternate-day dosing (e.g. 60-120 mg elemental) improves absorption. IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose, ferric derisomaltose) if short time, intolerance, malabsorption, or CKD; check phosphate with FCM.
- Add B12/folate if deficient, and consider ESA for CKD or refractory cases (with VTE caution). Avoid transfusing for a stable preop Hb; restrictive threshold (Hb ~7-8) is standard postop. TXA and cell salvage are the intraop PBM partners.

## Slide: Anaemia - interpretation and treatment
- Absolute iron deficiency: ferritin <30 ng/mL or TSAT <20%.
- With inflammation: ferritin <100 ng/mL may still indicate iron deficiency.
- IV iron is useful when surgery is soon, oral iron is ineffective or poorly tolerated.
- Hb <13 g/dL prompts assessment and treatment; elective timing is individualised.
**Added:**
- Ferritin high but TSAT low = anaemia of chronic disease / functional iron deficiency (iron present but locked by hepcidin); may still respond to IV iron. Low MCV/ MCH with raised RDW supports true iron deficiency; look for a cause (GI blood loss, NSAID use, malabsorption, menstrual loss) rather than just replacing iron.
- Response check: Hb should rise ~1 g/dL in 2-3 weeks on adequate iron; no response -> think non-adherence, malabsorption, ongoing loss, or other cause (B12/folate, CKD, marrow).
- IV iron dose from total iron deficit (Ganzoni: weight x (target Hb - actual Hb) x 2.4 + stores ~500 mg) or fixed dosing per product; cautions: avoid during active infection, watch for hypersensitivity/ infusion reactions, hypophosphataemia with ferric carboxymaltose.
- If Hb stays <13 despite treatment and surgery is elective, individualise (delay a few weeks vs proceed with PBM measures such as TXA).

## Slide: Kidney function - creatinine is not enough
- Record serum creatinine AND eGFR (CKD-EPI), electrolytes and urine albumin/creatinine ratio when relevant.
- eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for >=3 months suggests CKD; <30 is advanced CKD.
- Review potassium, fluid balance, nephrotoxins, NSAIDs and drug dosing.
- No single serum creatinine value is a universal THR/TKR cancellation cut-off.
- Footer: an eGFR trend and AKI risk are more useful than isolated creatinine.
**Added:**
- Creatinine alone misleads: low muscle mass (elderly, frail, women, malnourished) keeps creatinine "normal" while eGFR is low. CKD-EPI corrects for age and sex. Cystatin C-based eGFR is an option when muscle mass is unusual.
- CKD staging: G1 >=90, G2 60-89, G3a 45-59, G3b 30-44, G4 15-29, G5 <15; albuminuria (ACR) adds risk (A1 <30, A2 30-300, A3 >300 mg/g). CKD needs >=3 months of abnormality, so a single low value after dehydration or acute illness may be AKI instead.
- Why it matters: CKD raises risk of AKI, PJI, transfusion, readmission and mortality after arthroplasty; AKI is also a post-op risk from hypotension, NSAIDs, contrast and tourniquet/ cement-related events.
- Practical: avoid NSAIDs (use paracetamol-based multimodal analgesia), adjust antibiotics (e.g. vancomycin, aminoglycosides) and anticoagulant doses (enoxaparin, DOACs) to eGFR, avoid hypotension/ dehydration, correct K+, coordinate with nephrology for eGFR <30 (and dialysis timing for dialysis patients).

## Slide: Nutrition and obesity - practical thresholds
- Albumin <3.5 g/dL is a risk marker; assess inflammation, liver/renal disease and nutrition.
- BMI >=40 kg/m2 increases complications in many cohorts; no universal mandatory cut-off.
- Assess sarcopenia, weight loss, diet quality and protein intake, not BMI alone.
- Encourage weight and comorbidity optimisation without indefinite surgery delay.
- Refs: TJA optimisation narrative review 2022; ACR/AAHKS timing guideline 2023.
- Side panel: SARC-F screen for sarcopenia - Strength (lift/carry 10 lb), Assistance in walking, Rise from chair, Climb stairs (10 steps), Falls in past year. Scoring: none 0, some 1, a lot/unable 2 (falls: none 0, 1-3 falls 1, >=4 falls 2).
**Added:**
- SARC-F total 0-10; score >=4 predicts sarcopenia (high specificity, low sensitivity, so it misses some cases). Confirm with grip strength (<27 kg men, <16 kg women, EWGSOP2) and chair-stand test; muscle mass by DXA/BIA if needed.
- Albumin is a poor nutrition marker by itself (negative acute-phase reactant; falls with inflammation, liver/renal disease). Combine with weight-loss history, MUST/ MNA screen, pre-albumin/ lymphocyte count, vitamin D, and protein intake. Low albumin predicts more PJI, wound problems and longer stay after TJA.
- Obesity: BMI >=40 (and especially >=50) associated with higher infection, wound complications, revision and dislocation; also technical difficulty. Offer weight-loss support (diet, GLP-1 RA, bariatric surgery referral) but do not delay indefinitely, since pain and immobility themselves worsen health. Protein ~1.2-1.5 g/kg/day around surgery in at-risk patients, and prehab for sarcopenic patients.

## SARC-F and TKR - evidence (searched 20 Nov 2026)
- Systematic review of observational studies (J Clin Med 2026, PROSPERO CRD420261320013): sarcopenia prevalence in TKA 7.7-25%; sarcopenic patients had more medical events, transfusion, falls, fractures, reoperations and implant-related complications, longer stay and higher cost; PROM differences less consistent. Pooled prevalence in another meta-analysis ~20.1% (13.6-28.8).
- Matched-control TKA study (J Knee Surg): 180 sarcopenic vs 345 controls; KSS-C 83.0 vs 88.2, KSS-F 79.2 vs 86.1, complications 14.1% vs 4.1%; sarcopenic group had lower BMI, albumin and Hb.
- Lovett 2021 (Can Geriatr J): SARC-F used in OA patients 60+ at musculoskeletal centres; those assessed for joint replacement are at high risk of sarcopenia (esp. hip); at-risk patients more likely referred to ortho (OR 1.30).
- SARC-CalF / calf circumference (JGPT 2024, 599 pts >=65): ~29-32% at risk; useful for planning rehab in hip arthroplasty, accuracy may be low in knee arthroplasty.
- Sarcopenia also linked to PJI and longer LOS in TJA (Arthroplasty Today 2022); sarcopenic obesity (normal BMI can hide it).
- Peri-op protein/amino acid supplementation reduces muscle atrophy after TJA (meta-analysis 2025); post-TKA quadriceps atrophy/ weakness can persist up to ~5 months.
- My inference (not from a source): in knee OA, SARC-F items (stairs, rising from chair, walking) are also hit by joint pain, so it can over-call; confirm with grip strength/ chair-stand/ DXA-BIA. Evidence is observational; trials showing that treating sarcopenia preop improves TKR outcomes are lacking.

- Tourniquet (Devarsh, speaker): after applying the tourniquet, pull the tendons/soft tissue distally and hold them there.
  Added (my reading; verify vs speaker): the aim is to avoid the thigh soft tissue/ quadriceps being tethered proximally under the cuff, so the extensor mechanism stays mobile for exposure, patellar eversion and later flexion. Standard points: pad the thigh and place the cuff as proximal as possible, size cuff to limb, exsanguinate by elevation or Esmarch, then inflate. Pressure by limb occlusion pressure (+~50-75 mmHg) or ~100-150 mmHg above systolic (typically 250-300 mmHg thigh); aim to keep under ~90-120 min. Risks: nerve/ muscle injury, skin blisters, post-op thigh pain, DVT; some surgeons release before closure for haemostasis. Evidence on tourniquet vs no tourniquet: less blood loss intra-op, better cementing field, but no clear benefit on total blood loss and possibly slower early recovery.

### Prehab before TKA (user ask, Sun 22 Nov)
**Role (ad):** optimise patient before surgery: strength/ROM/walking, teach post-op exercises, aids and home set-up, plus medical optimisation (anaemia, glucose, BMI, smoking, BP) and expectation setting.
**Evidence (ad, from memory, not searched):** Cochrane-type reviews: low-certainty, small short-term gain in pain/function, no clear effect on LOS or long-term outcome. Benefit mostly for weak/frail pts; best value = education + medical optimisation. Confirm with speaker.
**Easy plan (ad):** 4-6 wks pre-op, daily: quad sets, SLR, heel slides, sit-to-stand from chair, glute bridges, ankle pumps; walk 20-30 min; stationary cycling if available; practise crutch/walker and stairs (good up, bad down); fix home (raised commode, remove rugs); stop smoking, control sugar, treat anaemia.
